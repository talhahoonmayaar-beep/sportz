Fence Calculator

Enter the total fence run and panel spacing to get the panel count, post count (gate posts included) and rail totals. Plan the order before the auger comes out.

Calculate posts and panels

Counts assume one continuous straight run. Each gate adds a latch-side post; corner and end posts should be heavier duty than line posts. Keep one spare panel for rip cuts and damage.

How the fence calculation works

Fence material counts are integer math, and the rounding matters. Posts and panels come in fixed widths, so the last bay almost always needs a cut panel — the count has to round up, not round to nearest.

Panels: total run ÷ panel width, rounded up. A 120 ft run of 6 ft panels: 120 ÷ 6 = 20 panels. A 100 ft run: 100 ÷ 6 = 16.67 → 17 panels, with the last one ripped to fit a 4-ft bay.

Posts: panels + 1 on a straight run (each panel needs a post on both ends). Gates: every gate adds one latch-side post, and gate posts should be set deeper and in concrete regardless of soil. The calculator also totals rails at two per bay for two-rail styles like split rail or welded-wire framing.

Rule of thumb
Walk the fence line with a 100-ft tape and write down every corner, gate and grade break before ordering. Corners and ends use heavier posts — count them separately on the plan, not at the lumberyard.

Planning the run: corners, gates and slopes

Corners: a straight run needs panels + 1 posts, but every corner converts two line posts into one heavier corner post — same count, different hardware. Gates: add one post per gate for the latch side; both gate posts carry the swing weight and get set 6 inches deeper in concrete. Slopes: measure along the slope, not the horizontal, and expect to either rack the panels (stepped rails follow the grade) or stair-step them (posts get taller, panels stay level). Racked panels cover slopes with standard counts; stepped fences add a foot or two of post height per step.

Frost: in freeze-thaw climates, set posts below the frost line or frost heave will jack them out of the ground within a couple of winters. That's why the "how deep" answer is a range: 24 inches works in much of the South, while northern states want 36–48 inches. Concrete should crown just above grade so water runs off the post instead of pooling against it.

Fence calculator FAQ

How far apart should fence posts be?

Match the panel width — 6–8 feet for most wood and vinyl fences, 8–10 feet for chain-link line posts. Tighten spacing on slopes, in high wind, or wherever the fence carries a gate.

How many posts do I need for a 100-foot fence?

17 posts at 6-foot spacing (17 bays rounded up plus the end post), plus one latch-side post per gate. Corners swap line posts for heavier corner posts without changing the count.

How deep should fence posts be set?

One-third of the above-ground height, 3 feet minimum where frost or soft ground is a factor. A 6-foot fence wants 2–3 feet of post in the ground, and gate posts go deeper still, always in concrete.

How many fence panels do I need?

Run length ÷ panel width, rounded up. The final bay usually takes a rip-cut panel — normal on every job. Keep one spare panel for cuts, splits and the occasional panel that arrives twisted.

How much concrete per fence post?

Two 50-pound bags for a 10-inch × 24-inch hole; three to four bags for 12 inches × 36 inches. Line posts in firm clay can dry-set with tamped gravel; gate and corner posts should always be concreted.
