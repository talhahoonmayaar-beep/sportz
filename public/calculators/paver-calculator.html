Calculate pavers for your project

Pallet counts use a conservative 100 pavers per pallet — actual pallets range from ~100 (16×16 slabs) to 500+ (4×8 bricks). Order full pallets where possible; mixed lots rarely match.

How the paver calculation works

Pavers flip the usual logic: instead of converting area to volume, you divide the paved area by the area of a single paver. The units have to agree, which is where most DIY errors creep in — pavers are sized in inches, areas in feet.

Step 1 — paver area in square inches: length × width. A 4 × 8 inch brick paver covers 32 sq in.

Step 2 — convert to pavers per square foot: 144 ÷ 32 = 4.5 pavers per sq ft. (144 is the number of square inches in a square foot.)

Step 3 — multiply by area, then add pattern and waste: a 12 × 14 ft patio is 168 sq ft → 168 × 4.5 = 756 pavers in stack bond. Running bond adds 5% for cuts, herringbone about 10%. With 10% total waste: 756 × 1.10 = 832 → order 835–840, or nine pallets on the conservative 100-per-pallet plan.

Rule of thumb
Pavers per square foot: 4×8 → 4.5, 6×6 → 4, 6×9 → 2.67, 12×12 → 1, 16×16 → 0.5625. Multiply by area, then add 5–10%.

Pattern choice changes the order

Stack bond (rows aligned) is the cleanest and cheapest: almost every paver lays whole, so 5% waste covers mistakes. Running bond (each row offset half a paver, like a brick wall) needs 10% — every row end is a cut. Herringbone looks bespoke and eats 10–15% in diagonal off-cuts, and it's also the strongest interlock, which is why it's the required pattern for driveway pavers in most manufacturer specs. Diagonal layouts of any pattern add another few percent because border cuts happen at every edge.

Mixed-size patterns — the "I pattern" and "basket weave" sold on pallet displays — are quoted by the manufacturer in square-foot bundles, not per-paver counts. If you're buying one of those kits, order by square footage plus 10% and ignore per-paver math entirely.

What sits under the pavers matters as much as the count

The paver count is only as good as the base. A proper install means digging 7–9 inches for a patio: 4–6 inches of compacted ¾-inch crushed gravel, a 1-inch bedding layer of coarse sand (never rounded play sand), then the pavers, then polymeric sand swept into the joints. Driveways double the base — 8–12 inches — because the loads are point-concentrated, and edges need restraint spike-in plastic edging or the field creeps apart within a season or two.

Order from a single manufacturing lot when possible. Concrete pavers vary slightly between production runs, and a pallet from a different lot can read visibly lighter or darker laid side by side. That's the strongest argument for over-ordering rather than topping up later — and why keeping 5–10 attic-stock pavers after the job is standard practice among installers.

Paver calculator FAQ

How many 4×8 pavers do I need for 100 square feet?

About 450 in stack bond (4.5 per sq ft × 100). With cuts and waste, plan on 470–500; herringbone pushes the order toward 500 because roughly 10% gets cut away.

How many pavers are in a pallet?

Depends entirely on the paver: 4×8 bricks ship 500–600 per pallet; 16×16 slabs only 96–120. The calculator's 100-per-pallet figure is deliberately conservative — confirm the supplier's actual pallet configuration before ordering.

How do I calculate pavers per square foot?

Multiply paver length × width in inches, then divide 144 by that figure. A 4×8 paver is 32 sq in: 144 ÷ 32 = 4.5 pavers per square foot. Multiply by area, add pattern waste, round up.

How much extra should I buy for cuts and waste?

5% stack bond on a plain rectangle, 10% running bond or borders, 10–15% herringbone or diagonal. Whatever the pattern, keep 5–10 full pavers after the job for future repairs — matching a discontinued lot later is nearly impossible.

How much base do I need under pavers?

Walkways: 4 inches of compacted gravel. Patios: 4–6 inches. Driveways: 8–12 inches. Add 1 inch of bedding sand on top and figure a 7–9 inch dig for a typical patio.
