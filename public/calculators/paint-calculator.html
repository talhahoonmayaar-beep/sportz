How the paint calculation works

Paint math is pure area: measure the surface, subtract what you're not painting, multiply by coats, divide by coverage. The calculator runs that chain for you, but here's the working so you can sanity-check any result.

Step 1 — wall area: perimeter × height = (length + width) × 2 × height. For a 12 × 12 ft room with 8 ft ceilings: (12 + 12) × 2 × 8 = 384 sq ft.

Step 2 — subtract openings: a standard 32 × 80 inch door eats about 21 sq ft; an average window, about 15 sq ft. Our example room with one of each: 384 − 21 − 15 = 348 sq ft.

Step 3 — multiply by coats and divide by coverage: two coats means 348 × 2 = 696 sq ft of coverage needed. At the typical label figure of 350 sq ft per gallon: 696 ÷ 350 = 1.99 gallons — buy 2, and grab a third if the walls are textured or you're making a dramatic color change.

Rule of thumb
One gallon covers a bathroom or closet in two coats. Two gallons cover a 12×12 bedroom. Three cover a 20×20 living room with 9-foot ceilings.

What changes real-world coverage

The label's 350–400 sq ft per gallon assumes smooth, sealed, light-colored surfaces. Real rooms bite into that number: texture (orange-peel and knockdown drywall can cut coverage 25%), porosity (fresh drywall joint compound drinks the first coat unless primed), and color contrast (deep reds and blues hide poorly even at premium prices). Cheap paint is often thin — lower solids mean fewer square feet per gallon no matter what the label claims.

Batching matters more than rounding. If two walls get one gallon and the other two get a different batch, the slight tint difference between batches shows as vertical stripes. Buy all your gallons at once, and if the count lands just over a round number (like 2.1), buy three rather than stretching two — a visible seam costs more than the spare gallon you return unopened.

Measuring tips that prevent short orders

Measure the room's length and width at floor level, then ceiling height at two corners — older homes rarely have level floors, and the taller corner wins. Count every door and window and round windows up to 15 sq ft if they're oversize. For open floor plans, don't sum rooms mechanically: measure the wall runs between corners and treat each continuous run as its own wall so shared openings aren't deducted twice. Closets count if they're getting the same color — a reach-in adds 60–80 sq ft.

Paint calculator FAQ

How much area does one gallon of paint cover?

350–400 square feet per coat on smooth, primed surfaces. Rough textures drop that to 250–300, and unprimed drywall or dramatic color changes consume more still. Check the specific can — coverage is printed on every label.

How many gallons do I need for a 12×12 room?

About 2 gallons for two coats: 384 sq ft of wall minus a door and window is roughly 348 net, doubled to 696 sq ft of coverage. Keep a third gallon on hand for textured walls or white-over-dark repaints.

Do I need two coats of paint?

Yes for virtually every real-world job. Two coats give even color and a scrub-able film; a single coat shows roller streaking and thin spots even over the same color. Only paint-and-primer products over a close color sometimes succeed with one, and a second coat still improves durability.

How much paint for the ceiling?

Ceiling area is just length × width — about 144 sq ft in a 12×12 room, far under a gallon. If the ceiling is the same color, fold it into the calculation with the checkbox. If it's a different color or a big room, buy ceiling paint separately: it's usually cheaper and formulated not to spatter.

How do I calculate the square footage of a wall?

Perimeter times height: (length + width) × 2 × height. Subtract 21 sq ft per standard door and 15 sq ft per window. Multiply by coats, divide by the can's coverage, round up.

Should I buy a 5-gallon pail?

For 15+ gallons of the same color, yes — pails batch perfectly and cost less per gallon. Below about 3 gallons, cans are easier to carry, pour and store. The calculator's "buy in" note switches automatically at the 5-gallon threshold.
